# Assessment practices for computing education in the age of generative AI

## Provisional synthesis of the assessment catalogue

This report applies the synthesis described in [the review methodology](methodology.md) to the current assessment catalogue. The catalogue contains 247 student-facing assessment records. The current mapping pass associates 40 records from 37 sources with 19 of the 59 practices identified in the educator workshop. Those records produce 61 assessment-to-practice mappings because one assessment can instantiate more than one practice. The remaining 40 practices have no candidate mapping at this stage.

These figures describe the present state of the catalogue, not the prevalence of practices in computing education. The LLM-assisted pass reviewed every canonical assessment record against all 59 practices. It assigned at least one candidate mapping to 40 records and found no direct match for the other 207. All candidate mappings await independent review. “No candidate mapping” therefore means that the completed mapping pass found no direct match in the current record. It does not establish absence from the published literature because extraction or classification may be incomplete. The catalogue's labels of strong, moderate, and limited refer to the completeness and source support of an assessment record. They do not constitute ratings of an intervention's effectiveness.

The synthesis distinguishes a description or proposal from an implementation report and an empirical evaluation. A source counts once within each practice even when it describes more than one assessment. Survey responses, student perceptions, and observed work patterns provide useful implementation evidence, but they do not by themselves establish gains in learning, validity, integrity, equity, or cost-effectiveness.

## Open-ended and authentic task design

**Open-ended task defined by what students must demonstrate.** Hammond and Faber's open-ended C project specifies a set of programming capabilities while allowing students to design their own text-adventure game. Students could use AI, had to document any prompts and responses, and submitted a narrated code walkthrough and demonstration ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-ai-permitted-text-adventure-project.json); [Hammond and Faber, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/FIE61694.2024.10892956)). The source establishes a concrete implementation but the catalogue contains no extracted learning or integrity outcome. The evidence supports feasibility in one 35-student course, not comparative effectiveness.

**Deliberately over-scope a project assuming AI use.** Filcik, Sobiesk, and Matthews required 544 first-year students to produce something beyond what the student or AI could accomplish alone. Students submitted a product, complete AI transcripts, work-in-progress evidence, a reflection, and a demonstration ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-cs0-genai-teaming-final-project.json); [Filcik et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3641554.3701853)). The study reports that 91% used ChatGPT and documents the range of products. It does not compare learning, creativity, workload, or project quality with a conventionally scoped assessment, so the central premise remains untested.

**Make project scoping a student responsibility.** Two sources represent this practice. Graduate students proposed a host-course GenAI tool, validated its responses, and obtained peer reviews in a two-week project; their mean self-rating for prompt-engineering skill rose from 3.3 to 4.9 ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-pbl-custom-genai-mini-project.json); [Kusam et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/FIE61694.2024.10893118)). A second source describes student choice within a large-codebase project, but its catalogue record has limited evidence. The first result measures self-perception after a broader instructional module and cannot attribute the change to student-led scoping.

**Require AI disclosure and citation.** Clift and Petrovska allowed final-year degree apprentices to use GenAI freely in a mobile application project but required citation of generated code, reflection, and a viva ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-mobile-app-freeform-genai-development.json); [Clift and Petrovska, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3702212.3702214)). All 13 students used GenAI and more than 80% of the 12 survey respondents viewed its inclusion positively. The small sample and absence of a comparison leave the effects of citation on attribution, validation, or learning unresolved.

The catalogue has no candidate mappings for **grade technical explanation rather than code alone**, **require decomposition before delegation to AI**, **open-ended data investigation with student-written tests**, or **compare multiple AI-generated designs**. These practices remain research gaps in the current synthesis. Mapping review should examine records involving explanations, testing, and comparative design before treating them as gaps in the literature itself.

## Ambitious AI-leveraged projects

**Use AI for ambitious authentic projects.** Shah and colleagues required upper-level students to add a feature to Python's 24,000-line `idlelib` codebase with GitHub Copilot ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-idlelib-go-to-definition-copilot.json); [Shah et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3641554.3701800)). Analysis of 48 process journals and 47 chat transcripts found that 63% attempted a one-shot prompt for the whole feature. This evidence describes student strategies in a realistic codebase, but it does not establish that AI enabled more ambitious work or improved learning.

**Grade problem framing, prompting, and model guidance.** Chen and colleagues' first-year network-engineering project awards 25% of interim marks to discussion and prompt formulation and 25% to AI engagement and critical evaluation ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-network-ipv6-genai-authentic-project.json); [Chen et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1109/EDUCON62633.2025.11016382)). The pilot establishes a detailed rubric and implementation. The catalogue records student feedback and assessment outputs as instruments but contains no extracted result, leaving reliability and the relation between prompt quality and disciplinary understanding open.

**Combine project deliverables with oral and qualitative verification.** Five sources combine an artifact with a presentation, demonstration, report, or oral examination. Examples include the IPv6 project, the CS0 teaming project, the C text-adventure project, and five-minute code interviews attached to weekly programming assignments. Kannam and colleagues used surveys, observations, focus groups, and staff interviews across two quarters; spring proficiency ratings were 30% Exemplary, 63% Satisfactory, and 7% Approaching ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-code-interviews.json); [Kannam et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3641554.3701806)). This is one of the better represented practices, but the studies do not isolate the contribution of oral verification or compare validity, equity, and staff cost against artifact-only grading.

**Use explanation, alternatives, and counterfactual questions.** The code-interview study asks students to trace execution, compare approaches, find bugs, suggest alternatives, and solve an isomorphic problem. Its two-quarter action-research design provides implementation and proficiency data, but no independent criterion establishes whether these questions measure understanding more accurately than other oral formats.

The current mapping contains no example of **using AI to handle unfamiliar experimental tooling**, **deriving specifications and implementing them with coding agents**, **providing provider-independent or institution-supported AI access**, **offering small self-hosted models for ethical or privacy concerns**, **using recurring presentations for project checkpoints**, or **assessing agent-management skills**. Their absence is notable because access, privacy, and agent management concern the conditions under which an assessment operates as much as its task design. The present catalogue emphasizes completed student tasks and may under-record infrastructure and curriculum-level arrangements.

## Evaluating and fixing AI-produced work

**Give students AI-generated solutions to critique, improve, or test.** Five assessment records from three sources represent this practice. Petrovska and colleagues asked first- and final-year degree apprentices to inspect ChatGPT-generated Java and Dart work and compare it with prior work; the Dart task also asked students to apply the original coursework rubric ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-apprentice-dart-snooker-chatgpt-critique.json); [Petrovska et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3633053.3633057)). Pu integrated comparison, debugging, optimization, and validation tasks into six CS2 homeworks; among 47 survey respondents, 85.1% agreed that GenAI helped with testing and debugging and 74.5% agreed that it helped with implementation ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-cs2-genai-homework-series.json); [Pu, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/FIE61694.2024.10892822)). These results support implementability and positive perceptions, but they do not measure students' ability to detect defects or transfer critique skills.

**Assess analysis of generated work rather than code production.** Two formative critique assessments appear in the same Petrovska et al. source. Students analyzed generated Java and Dart work; the Dart task asked them to apply the original coursework rubric, while the Java task asked them to answer critique questions and rate the code. The catalogue contains no extracted outcome, and one source cannot establish whether analysis provides a valid substitute for code production across learner levels or topics.

**Deliberately generate flawed AI code for critique.** The BugSpotter assessment uses generated debugging exercises designed around flawed solutions ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-bugspotter-test-case-exercises.json); [Feng et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3716640.3716658)). The mapped record establishes implementation but contains no extracted empirical result. Open questions include control over defect type and difficulty, the possibility of accidental ambiguity, and whether generated faults produce better debugging practice than curated faults.

No current mapping represents **scaffolding responsibility for prompting and evaluation**, **peer review of AI co-produced work**, **using a difficult critique task to motivate fundamentals**, **pairing AI-supported coursework with independent verification**, or **retaining a final proctored fundamentals assessment**. Some catalogue assessments contain peer review or controlled components, so these gaps require practice-specific remapping before interpretation.

## Controlled and AI-free assessment

**Require students to begin without AI.** Four sources require an unaided phase before a later AI-supported phase. In software-testing education, students first produced testing artifacts manually and then recreated them with ChatGPT or Copilot ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-software-testing-manual-ai-comparison.json); [Haldar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3545882)). The authors analyzed 47 submissions selected from 96 because they scored at least 90%; students concluded that LLMs could augment but not replace manual testing. Selection of high-scoring work limits generalization, and the design does not isolate whether the initial unaided phase improved later evaluation. The other mapped sources add implementations but little comparative evidence.

The catalogue does not yet represent **moving compromised learning outcomes to proctored AI-free assessment**, **using post-submission interviews**, **requiring diagrams or video instead of written text**, **assessing innovation, originality, and novelty**, **using controlled digital entry for machine-grading**, **using a flipped classroom for supervised assignment work**, **using micro-milestones and version-control history**, or **using self-reflection as a preliminary authenticity check**. The lack of a post-submission-interview mapping should be read with care: the code-interview record is mapped to narrower oral practices, which shows how inventory boundaries affect apparent coverage.

## Process evidence and effort signals

**Collect process evidence.** Nine sources require material such as prompts, responses, journals, screenshots, logs, or reflections. The examples span introductory projects, data structures, data science, security, networking, concurrent programming, and large-codebase work. Shah et al. analyzed journals and transcripts rather than treating them only as integrity evidence. Browning and colleagues jointly graded a data-analysis report and a prompt-engineering report that documented prompts, responses, and critical evaluation ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-data-science-dual-report-genai.json); [Browning et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/FIE61694.2024.10893452)). This is broad evidence of adoption, but the studies use different process artifacts and purposes. The catalogue does not establish which traces are valid indicators of learning, how students may curate them, or whether collection burdens and privacy risks are proportionate.

**Use a three-part deliverable, interaction-log, and reflection submission.** The 544-student CS0 project combines a product, complete AI transcripts, and critical reflection, together with a short demonstration. Reported results describe tool uptake and product types rather than the validity of the three-part design. Research still needs to compare selective interaction evidence with full transcript submission and assess grading cost, student privacy, and the quality of reflection.

**Embed process in an engineering design lifecycle.** Keuning and colleagues propose a GenAI-centered CS1 lifecycle project that creates points at which instructors can observe process ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-genai-centered-cs1-lifecycle-project.json); [Keuning et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3699538.3699591)). The source provides a design rather than implementation evidence. Feasibility, learner support, and assessment reliability therefore remain unknown.

**Assess structured decomposition explicitly** has no candidate mapping. Process logs may expose decomposition, but collecting evidence of a process and grading decomposition as a competence are distinct practices. The present catalogue supports the former and not the latter.

## Rubric and grading redesign

**Make AI-aware submission requirements part of the rubric.** Three sources connect AI-related evidence to marks. Pu allocates 10% of each homework to the GenAI activity; Browning et al. jointly assess project and prompt-engineering reports; Chen et al. grade prompt formulation, engagement, critical evaluation, and validated process evidence. These sources establish workable rubric designs across CS2, postgraduate data science, and first-year networking. One study reports student perceptions and the others provide qualitative or initial evaluation, but none tests inter-rater reliability or whether rubric scores predict disciplinary understanding.

No candidate mapping currently represents **standards-, mastery-, or specifications-based grading**, **mastery or fluency checks**, **reserving high-stakes credit for directly verifiable work**, **hidden rubric criteria**, or **tiered grading with optional AI-integrated work**. The evidence base therefore offers more examples of adding AI-specific criteria to conventional grading than of changing the grading model itself. Hidden criteria also raise transparency and fairness questions that require evaluation rather than implementation evidence alone.

## Oral and interactive assessment

**Require real-time modification or extension during oral assessment.** Kannam et al.'s code interviews require tracing, debugging, comparison, alternatives, or code for an isomorphic problem. The action-research study demonstrates delivery to a course cohort of about 200 with five-minute interviews and a four-level rubric. It reports proficiency distributions and implementation experience, but does not isolate live modification from the other interview elements or provide a measure of authorship detection.

No candidate mapping represents **scripted oral assessments with question-level rubrics**, **small-group oral assessment**, **generating oral questions from each student's implementation**, **distributing oral or code-review assessment across calibrated teaching assistants**, **sampling students for unpredictable oral verification**, **downweighting the artifact and upweighting oral verification**, or **generating personalized questions with local AI models**. The code-interview study addresses adjacent design and scaling issues, but its mapping supports only those actions documented in the record. Evidence on assessor calibration, accessibility, anxiety, consistency, and workload remains sparse in the mapped corpus.

## Fairness, trust, and motivation

**Use open-book exams, retakes, or application-focused questions.** Bhalerao describes midterm and final examinations in which students could read internet material but could not communicate or use an LLM ([catalogue record](site/public/data/assessments/assessment-open-web-midterm-final-no-llm.json); [Bhalerao, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626253.3635511)). The record supports the open-web design but contains no extracted outcome and has limited evidence quality. It does not address retakes or compare application-focused questions with conventional exam items.

**Clearly communicate permitted and prohibited AI uses.** This is the most widely represented practice, with 20 assessment records from 19 sources. Policies range from unrestricted use with citation in the mobile application project, through task-specific permission in the text-adventure project, to prohibition in proctored Python midterms and in Python quizzes without ChatGPT access. The breadth shows that published assessments document boundaries at course, assignment, and component level. Some associated studies report student perceptions, observed use, or integrity-classification performance, but none directly tests whether clearer communication improves compliance, trust, learning, or equitable access. Policy clarity is therefore common in the mapped literature but not well evaluated as an intervention.

The catalogue has no candidate mapping for **shifting greater weight toward controlled assessments**, **using exam questions that mirror job interviews**, **building student ownership through explicit purpose and reflection**, or **using small-group meta-conversations about learning**. The final three practices concern student motivation and the meaning attached to assessment. Their absence may reflect the review's student-facing assessment unit, which captures graded tasks more readily than surrounding conversations and explanations.

## Overall state of the evidence

The current catalogue shows the strongest convergence between educator practice and published examples in four areas: explicit AI-use policies, collection of process evidence, combined artifact and oral or qualitative verification, and critique of generated work. Even in these areas, most studies establish that instructors implemented a design and that students could complete it. The outcomes tend to consist of perception surveys, usage frequencies, proficiency distributions, or qualitative observations. Few sources use a comparator that can attribute an outcome to a specific practice, and the mapped records contain no basis for ranking the practices by effectiveness.

The evidence is thinner for grading models, controlled assessment strategies, oral-assessment scaling, equitable access, privacy-preserving provision, and motivation. Some practices may be present in source details that the structured extraction or completed mapping pass did not capture. Others may occur in course materials or institutional practice before publication. Human review of the extraction and candidate mappings should precede claims about research gaps.

Three priorities follow from this provisional synthesis. First, reviewers should validate the 61 candidate mappings and re-examine records with no candidate match for the 40 uncovered practices. Second, source-level synthesis should extract missing samples, outcomes, limitations, and implementation costs before assigning evidence judgments. Third, future studies should evaluate the assessment claim attached to each practice. Relevant outcomes include learning and transfer for critique tasks, validity and reliability for process and oral evidence, compliance and trust for policy communication, and access, privacy, workload, and accessibility across all designs.
